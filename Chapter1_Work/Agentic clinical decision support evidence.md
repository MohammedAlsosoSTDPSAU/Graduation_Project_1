# Verified evidence narrows what Chapter 1 can claim

The verified literature supports a clear Chapter 1 argument, though not the one most agentic CDS proposals make. It shows that classical CDSS work best for prescribing safety and break down through alert fatigue, maintenance burden and automation bias. Single LLMs that score **67.6–86.5% on exam-style MedQA** lose about **10–20 accuracy points** when they have to gather information themselves. They also invent tools and references, overstate their confidence and change answers when the same facts arrive in a different order. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) gives modest gains but still leaves **about 30% of statements unsupported even with web retrieval**. Evaluation practice is dominated by accuracy (**95.4% of 519 studies**), and calibration is measured in only **1.2%**. What the literature does **not** show is that LLM multi-agent systems beat strong or compute-matched single agents in clinical decision support. Peer-reviewed general-domain work finds that a strongly prompted single agent nearly matches multi-agent discussion. The only clinical agent review that could be verified (a preprint) found **no head-to-head multi-agent vs single-agent comparison among its 20 included studies**. The regulatory and institutional documents (FDA CDS guidance of January 2026, WHO, NIST AI RMF, DECIDE-AI, SFDA, SDAIA) set boundaries: HCP-facing, a basis the clinician can review, and human oversight. They say nothing about whether a system works. Chapter 1 should therefore present the project as a test: do deterministic orchestration, pre-LLM safety checks, claim-to-source verification and a five-status output improve safety-relevant behaviour against fair baselines? It should not present the architecture as already shown to be better. Several widely repeated figures are still secondary or unverified and must be checked before citation: the 49–96% alert override range, the pooled OR of 1.35 for medical RAG, the MetaMedQA numbers and the Almanac numbers. Two readings of the FDA guidance's wording on time-critical decisions also conflict.

## Every citation below carries its verification level

The researchers could not open PubMed and PMC pages, which returned reCAPTCHA walls, and ScienceDirect, OUP and Crossref were partly blocked. Many sources were therefore read only as abstracts or official record pages. "Abstract-only" says what can be quoted from a source, not how good it is. For those sources, quote only the numbers in the abstract. The labels below are attached to every citation in this report.

| Label | Meaning | What you may do with it in Chapter 1 |
|---|---|---|
| **FT** | Full text (or a named large part of it) was read | Cite findings and numbers; still check verbatim quotes character by character against the PDF, because the fetch tool returned model-made extractions |
| **ABS** | Only the abstract, official landing page or record page was read | Cite only what the abstract states; no figures from tables or the body |
| **SEC** | The detail was seen only on a secondary page (blog, news, another review) | Do **not** cite the figure until the primary source is checked |
| **UNV** | The source could not be opened, or only its existence was confirmed | Do not cite content; at most list it as "to be confirmed" |

| **REG** | Regulatory, policy or guidance document | Use for definitions and boundaries only, never as evidence of effectiveness |

## Classical CDSS help most with prescribing and fail through alert fatigue and over-reliance

The standard peer-reviewed definition comes from Sutton et al.'s narrative review in *npj Digital Medicine*. A CDSS "is intended to improve healthcare delivery by enhancing medical decisions with targeted clinical knowledge, patient information, and other health information." The review splits CDSS into **knowledge-based systems**, which evaluate IF-THEN rules against patient data, and **non-knowledge-based systems**, which use AI, machine learning or statistical pattern recognition instead of programmed expert knowledge ([Sutton et al. 2020, FT](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1038/s41746-020-0221-y.pdf)). Sutton dates computer-based CDSS to the 1970s and describes them as now delivered mainly through EHRs and computerized physician order entry (CPOE). It reports that 41% of US hospitals with an EHR had a CDSS in 2013; that figure is second-hand from the review's own references. The AHRQ PSNet primer defines CDSS as "any system designed to improve clinical decision-making related to diagnostic or therapeutic processes of care" and says that by 2017 **more than 90% of US hospitals and 80% of clinics** had EHRs with some CDS. It describes explicit rules being replaced by machine learning and adds that "acceptance of artificial intelligence–based CDSS will require transparency around the methods used" ([AHRQ PSNet primer, FT of web page; last reviewed 2025](https://psnet.ahrq.gov/primer/clinical-decision-support-systems)). The primer's suggested-citation text says 2019 while the page says "last reviewed 2025", so cite the reviewed date plus an access date. **Neither source mentions LLMs.** The step from ML-based CDS to generative or LLM-based support has to be sourced from WHO's large multi-modal model guidance and the LLM literature discussed below.

The documented failure modes are specific. Sutton reports that **up to 95% of CDSS alerts are inconsequential**, which drives alert fatigue. Its other risks are fragmented workflows that add cognitive load; **automation bias** and reduced checking by clinicians; maintenance burden as knowledge bases fall behind practice; poor data quality (71% of patients with a splenectomy recorded in the EHR lacked it on their problem list); weak interoperability; cost (**up to 74% of users** reported that financial viability remained a struggle); and user distrust. One recommended mitigation for distrust is to reference expert sources inside alerts ([Sutton et al. 2020, FT](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1038/s41746-020-0221-y.pdf)). Sutton is a narrative review, so these numbers come from the primary studies it cites and are not pooled estimates. AHRQ adds an evidence-scope point that Chapter 1 should keep: CPOE with integrated CDSS "reliably prevent[s] prescribing errors," but current systems "are not effective at preventing errors at other stages of the medication use process," and the evidence for uses such as diagnostic accuracy is weaker ([AHRQ PSNet, FT of web page](https://psnet.ahrq.gov/primer/clinical-decision-support-systems)).

The research behind this section did not establish several things. The often-quoted range of **49–96% of drug-safety alerts overridden**, usually attributed to van der Sijs et al. (JAMIA 2006), is **UNV**: the citation exists, but the figure was not read ([PSNet record of van der Sijs 2006, citation only](https://psnet.ahrq.gov/issue/overriding-drug-safety-alerts-computerized-physician-order-entry)). The "five rights" of CDS (Osheroff) were not verified. No systematic review or meta-analysis of CDS effects on processes or outcomes was opened, so there is no verified basis for an unqualified "CDSS improve patient outcomes." The mapping from these failure modes to the project's components is the project's own argument, not a literature finding. Alert fatigue maps to centralized filtering and prioritisation, maintenance burden to retrieval of current guidelines, distrust to cited and verified claims, and automation bias to physician final authority. A defensible framing that follows from Sutton's taxonomy is that the project is a **hybrid of the two classical CDSS types**: knowledge-based deterministic rules and decision core, plus non-knowledge-based LLM agents and retrieval.

## Single LLMs ace exams but falter when they must gather, weigh and abstain

### Benchmark capability is real and must be acknowledged

Chapter 1 should not claim that LLMs are bad at medicine. Flan-PaLM reached **67.6% on MedQA (USMLE-style)**, more than 17 points above the previous state of the art ([Singhal et al., Nature 2023, FT](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10396962)). Med-PaLM 2 reached **86.5%**, and physicians preferred its answers to physician-written answers on 8 of 9 axes across 1,066 consumer questions ([Singhal et al., arXiv v1 2023, ABS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.09617)). Med-PaLM 2 has since been published in *Nature Medicine* (2025, DOI 10.1038/s41591-024-03423-7) ([Nature Index record, ABS](https://www.nature.com/nature-index/article/10.1038/s41591-024-03423-7)), but the published figures, volume and pages were not verified. On 302 hard NEJM case reports, AMIE alone achieved **59.1% top-10 differential accuracy against 33.6% for unassisted clinicians** ([McDuff et al., Nature 2025, ABS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v642y2025i8067d10.1038_s41586-025-08869-4.html)). In a randomized trial of 50 physicians, GPT-4 alone scored 16 points higher than physicians using conventional resources. That was an exploratory secondary analysis, and physicians given GPT-4 did **not** improve significantly: 76% vs 74%, P = .60 ([Goh et al., JAMA Netw Open 2024, ABS](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/large-language-model-influence-on-diagnostic-reasoning-a-randomiz/)). A *Nature Medicine* review sums up the field as "impressive but mixed results" ([Thirunavukarasu et al. 2023, ABS](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-023-02448-8.pdf)). Its body text was not read, so it should not be cited for specific claims about hallucination, privacy or outdated knowledge.

Even the strongest early results carry safety signals. In long-form human evaluation, the instruction-tuned Med-PaLM produced **incorrect or inappropriate content in 18.7% of answers vs 1.4% for clinicians**, and omitted important content in 15.3% vs 11.1%. The authors wrote that it "remains inferior to clinicians" ([Singhal et al. 2023, FT](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10396962)). The pro-LLM results come from curated, complete case descriptions scored on diagnosis alone. The negative results below come from settings that require gathering information, following guidelines and staying robust to how the case is presented. The two literatures do not contradict each other; they measure different things, and that difference is the research gap.

### Realistic decision-making exposes information-gathering and robustness failures

The central study for 1.2 is Hager et al. in *Nature Medicine* 2024 ([Hager et al., FT via Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41591-024-03097-1); [accepted version, FT](https://push-zb.helmholtz-munich.de/deliver.php?id=35883)). It uses **MIMIC-CDM: 2,400 real emergency-department patients with acute abdominal pain**, covering appendicitis, cholecystitis, pancreatitis and diverticulitis. The LLMs had to request examinations, labs and imaging through tools and then diagnose and propose treatment. They diagnosed **significantly worse than physicians (P < 0.001), roughly 16–25 points lower**. Exact per-pathology values from Fig. 2 still need checking. Mean accuracy fell when models had to gather information themselves rather than receive it all: Llama 2 Chat **58.8% → 45.5%**, OASST 67.8% → 54.9%, WizardLM 65.1% → 53.9%. No model consistently ordered the required lab categories; OASST ordered pancreatic enzymes for pancreatitis in only 56.5% of cases. Treatment recommendations often missed guideline-indicated interventions. Models made **action-formatting errors every 2–4 patients and invented non-existent tools every 2–5 patients**. **Reordering the same information shifted accuracy by up to 18 points**, small rewordings by up to about 16, and less information sometimes beat full information. The authors conclude that current LLMs are not ready for autonomous clinical decision-making. The scope limit matters: Hager tested **only Llama-2-70B-based open models** (quantized, 4,096-token context), because MIMIC data-use rules prevented testing GPT-4. It covers four pathologies at one US hospital, and the clinician comparison used four hospitalists on 80 patients. One extraction said 100 cases, so confirm the number.

Two other lines of evidence point the same way. CRAFT-MD turned 2,000 vignettes into simulated doctor–patient conversations. **GPT-4 fell from 0.820 to 0.627** on four-option questions when it had to elicit the history, and **from 0.486 to 0.264** in free response. Experts judged that GPT-4 gathered a complete relevant history in only **71.11%** of conversations ([Johri et al., Nature Medicine 2025, FT via third-party mirror; cite the journal version](https://gwern.net/doc/ai/nn/transformer/gpt/4/nonfiction/2025-johri.pdf)). MediQ (NeurIPS 2024 poster) found that LLMs are trained to answer even when context is incomplete. Simply prompting them to ask questions **degraded** performance, while abstention strategies improved diagnostic accuracy by **22.3%** and still fell short of the full-information upper bound ([Li et al. 2024, ABS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.00922)). AgentClinic reports that in a sequential format, MedQA diagnostic accuracy can drop "to below a tenth" of static accuracy ([Schmidgall et al., PREPRINT, ABS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07960)). Together these studies justify a decision core that can return **NEED_MORE_INFO** as a first-class outcome, separate from answering.

### Hallucination, overconfidence, inconsistency and bias are measured, but on older models

Med-HALT, built from multinational medical exams, found that no 2023-era model reached acceptable accuracy on its False Confidence Test (best 42.21%). On memory tests (PubMed ID, title and link retrieval), the best average was 30.36%, and GPT-3.5 scored **0.29% on PMID-to-title** ([Pal et al., CoNLL 2023, FT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.15343); [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2023.conll-1.21)). Of 115 references that ChatGPT-3.5 generated for medical papers, **47% were fabricated, 46% were real but inaccurate, and 7% were correct** ([Bhattacharyya et al., Cureus 2023, FT](https://assets.cureus.com/uploads/original_article/pdf/158289/20230618-23404-wiiz34.pdf)). This applies to GPT-3.5 only and must not be generalized to current models. On calibration, MetaMedQA (12 models) found that models "consistently failed to recognize their knowledge limitations" and answered confidently even when the correct option was absent ([Griot et al., Nature Communications 2025, ABS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/natcom/v16y2025i1d10.1038_s41467-024-55628-6.html)). The commonly quoted numbers (GPT-4o 73.3% accuracy, unknown-recall 3.7%) are **SEC** from a news report and must not be cited yet ([News-Medical, SEC](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20250115/Study-reveals-AIe28099s-critical-flaw-in-medical-decision-making.aspx)). In medical diagnosis and treatment tasks, **verbalized confidence consistently overestimated confidence**. Sample consistency was the best proxy tested, with ROC AUC 0.66–0.79 ([Savage et al., JAMIA 2025, ABS](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1093%2FJAMIA%2FOCAE254)). Early evidence for deferral comes from Singhal et al.: deferring low-agreement answers raised MedQA accuracy to 82.5% at a 0.45 deferral fraction, a result the authors called "preliminary" ([Singhal et al. 2023, FT](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10396962)).

On bias and run-to-run inconsistency, ChatGPT, GPT-4, Bard and Claude **all produced race-based medical content** (eGFR, lung capacity, skin thickness), sometimes with invented equations. The authors note that "the concerning outputs do not always appear in all five runs of the same question" ([Omiye et al., npj Digital Medicine 2023, FT](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-023-00939-z.pdf)). The paper is internally inconsistent about question count: 9 in the text, 8 in the abstract. Flan-PaLM answers were rated biased in 7.9% of cases, Med-PaLM in 0.8% and clinicians in 1.4% ([Singhal et al. 2023, FT](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10396962)).

The research did not establish four things. No verified study tests LLMs on **explicitly conflicting** patient data, such as discordant labs and history; order and quantity sensitivity is the closest evidence. No study replicating MIMIC-CDM with GPT-4o-class or reasoning models was found. No study reporting a test-retest agreement statistic for clinical LLM decisions was found. "Outdated parametric knowledge" follows logically from training cut-offs but has no verified empirical quote here. The seed systematic review in *Discover Artificial Intelligence* (DOI suffix s44163-025-00784-x) is **UNV**: only its title and DOI string were seen ([Springer listing, UNV](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44163-025-00784-x)).

## Multi-agent gains are real against weak baselines and unproven against strong ones

### Foundations define the design space without ranking it

ReAct established the agent pattern of interleaving reasoning with tool actions. It reported **+34 points absolute success on ALFWorld and +10 on WebShop**, less hallucination and error propagation than chain-of-thought on HotpotQA and FEVER (no numbers given on the page), and improved human interpretability of trajectories ([Yao et al., ICLR 2023, ABS](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2023/oral/12647)). Guo et al.'s IJCAI-24 survey describes cooperative, debate and competitive paradigms, and layered, decentralised, centralised and shared-message-pool structures. It **gives no verdict on which structure is better**. It warns that "one agent's hallucination can have a cascading effect," calls for controlling information flow between agents, and notes that multi-agent scaling laws "remain unexplored" ([Guo et al., IJCAI 2024, FT](https://ijcai.org/proceedings/2024/890.md)). In this taxonomy the project's design falls between "centralised" and "shared message pool," with one difference: the coordinator is a deterministic state machine, not an LLM. That difference is a nameable design contribution, not a tested advantage. The Wang et al. agent survey exists ([arXiv, ABS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.11432)), but its journal details and framework content were not verified.

### Clinical multi-agent studies report gains, mostly on QA and case reports with GPT-4

MedAgents runs training-free, role-playing expert discussion to consensus across nine medical QA datasets, zero-shot. Its numeric results were not read ([Tang et al., Findings of ACL 2024, ABS](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-acl.33)). MDAgents assigns solo or group collaboration according to case complexity. It was best on **7 of 10 benchmarks, up to +4.2% over the previous best**, and moderator review plus external knowledge added **11.8%** on average ([Kim et al., NeurIPS 2024, ABS](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/90d1fc07f46e31387978b88e7e057a31-Abstract.html)). It is telling that this design routes simpler cases to a single agent for efficiency, which treats multi-agent collaboration as costly and not always needed. Ke et al. is the closest published analogue to a critic agent. On **16 cases** with GPT-4 Turbo agents, including a devil's advocate against anchoring, the initial diagnosis was correct in 0/80 runs. Final accuracy reached **64%, 69% and 76%** across configurations, against 48% for three physicians ([Ke et al., JMIR 2024, FT](https://jmir.org/2024/1/e59439)). The authors list small size, published cases that may appear in training data, excluded images and a fifth agent that "did not participate effectively" as limitations. Chen et al.'s multidisciplinary-team-style MAC framework beat single GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 and also chain-of-thought, Self-Refine and Self-Consistency on 302 rare-disease cases, "with higher performance and more output tokens." That comparison was **not compute-matched** ([Chen et al., npj Digital Medicine 2025, ABS reconstructed from OpenAlex](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1038%2FS41746-025-01550-0)). In realistic agentic EHR work, the best model reached only **69.67% task success** on 300 physician-written FHIR tasks ([MedAgentBench, PREPRINT, ABS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.14654)).

### Null and negative evidence against strong single agents is substantial

The peer-reviewed general-domain evidence is sceptical. Across reasoning tasks and backbones, "a single-agent LLM with strong prompts can achieve almost the same best performance" as the best multi-agent discussion. Multi-agent discussion won only when the prompt had no demonstrations ([Wang et al., ACL 2024, ABS](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.331)). "Multi-agent debating systems, in their current form, do not reliably outperform other proposed prompting strategies, such as self-consistency and ensembling," and they are more sensitive to hyperparameters ([Smit et al., ICML 2024, ABS](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/smit24a.html)). An earlier title suggested medical QA datasets, but the venue page did not confirm that. In medicine, MedAgentBoard found that multi-agent collaboration "does not consistently outperform advanced single LLMs" (for example on textual medical QA), and conventional specialised methods generally did better on medical VQA and EHR prediction ([Zhu et al., PREPRINT; NeurIPS 2025 listing not opened](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.12371v2)). The MAST taxonomy documents **14 failure modes** in system design, inter-agent misalignment and task verification, from over 1,600 annotated traces (inter-annotator kappa 0.88), on non-clinical tasks ([Cemri et al., PREPRINT, ABS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13657v3)). A controlled scaling study of **180 configurations** found that under fixed compute, multi-agent overhead disproportionately hurts tool-heavy tasks. Coordination gives diminishing or negative returns once the single-agent baseline exceeds about 45%, and every multi-agent variant **degraded sequential reasoning by 39–70%**. Error amplification was **17.2× for independent agents vs 4.4× with centralised coordination** ([Kim et al., PREPRINT, ABS; v3 not read](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.08296v2)).

The one clinical agent review that could be verified fits this picture. Gorenshtein et al. included 20 studies. **65% depended on synthetic data, all 20 were single-centre**, and real patient data appeared in only 2 studies (117 patients). It reported a median gain of **+36% over baseline LLMs**. By system type, single-agent tool calling showed a median of +53.0%, against +14.05% and +17.17% for the multi-agent types. These are medians across different tasks, not head-to-head results. The review states that "no study provides a comparison of multi-agent to single-agent" ([Gorenshtein et al., medRxiv 2025, PREPRINT, FT](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.08.22.25334232.full.pdf)). It also reports an inverted-U pattern with the best results at 4–5 agents, heavily caveated, and notes that cost and runtime were rarely reported and latency never was. Its exclusion counts are internally inconsistent: they add to 237, but 254 − 20 = 234. It may be indexed on PubMed (PMID 40909853 / PMC12407621), but cite it as a medRxiv preprint. The second seed review (ScienceDirect PII S1568494625017600) is **UNV** in every detail ([ScienceDirect URL, UNV](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1568494625017600)).

Read together, the clinical gains are measured against **single-pass LLMs or simple prompting, rarely against a tool-using single agent on the same budget**. The only quantitative evidence on centralised vs decentralised coordination is a non-clinical preprint. **Deterministic orchestration specifically has not been tested anywhere in this evidence.** For the project, a negative or null result against a compute-matched single-agent baseline is a plausible outcome that the literature predicts. Chapter 1 should present such a result as a legitimate finding, not a failure.

## Retrieval improves accuracy modestly but citations often fail to support claims

RAG was introduced as a way to combine parametric memory with a retrieved non-parametric index, partly because "providing provenance" and "updating knowledge" were open problems ([Lewis et al., NeurIPS 2020, ABS](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2020/hash/6b493230205f780e1bc26945df7481e5-Abstract.html)). In healthcare it is now widespread. A PRISMA review of **70 studies** (2020 to February 2025) found that 78.9% used English datasets and proprietary GPT models dominated. It reported a "lack of standardised evaluation frameworks," noted that most studies did not assess ethics or safety, and observed a trend toward modular and agentic designs ([Amugongo et al., PLOS Digital Health 2025, FT of first ~45%; Discussion not read](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12157099/)). Four authors are employed by Boehringer Ingelheim, which should be disclosed if the review is cited heavily. On the MIRAGE benchmark (7,663 questions, 41 configurations), MedRAG improved six LLMs by **up to 18% over chain-of-thought**. **Combining corpora and retrievers performed best**, and "lost-in-the-middle" position effects appeared ([Xiong et al., Findings of ACL 2024, ABS](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-acl.372)). Reciprocal Rank Fusion (k = 60) beat Condorcet and CombMNZ by about 4–5% on TREC tasks. That is general information retrieval, not clinical guidelines ([Cormack et al., SIGIR 2009, FT](https://cormack.uwaterloo.ca/cormacksigir09-rrf.pdf)). The JAMIA meta-analysis by Liu, McCoy and Wright (2025, DOI 10.1093/jamia/ocaf008) reports a **pooled OR of 1.35 (95% CI 1.19–1.53)** across 20 studies. That figure is **SEC**, seen only on a blog, and the outcome being pooled is unknown ([iatrox blog, SEC](https://www.iatrox.com/blog/rag-medical-ai-jamia-meta-analysis-20-studies-iatrox)). Another paper by the same group cites the review, which confirms it exists ([Liu et al. JAMIA 2025, FT](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12089757/)).

The faithfulness evidence is the strongest support for the project's claim-to-source verifier. SourceCheckup tested seven LLMs on about **58,000 statement–source pairs**. **50–90% of responses were not fully supported by their cited sources**, and some were contradicted by them. **Even GPT-4o with Web Search left about 30% of statements unsupported**, and nearly half of its responses were not fully supported. Doctors' independent assessments corroborated the results ([Wu et al., Nature Communications 2025, ABS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/natcom/v16y2025i1d10.1038_s41467-025-58551-6.html)). A 2026 retrieve-then-verify study is methodologically closest to the project. It retrieved trial-report passages with BM25 for each claim and assigned one of four verdicts (Supported, Contradicted, Not Found, Out of Scope). For GPT-5, o3-mini and GPT-3.5 doing risk-of-bias assessment on 97 RCTs, **binary accuracy reached 90–98%, yet evidence support was only 60–65% and conservative hallucination rates were 34–37%** ([Liang et al., JMIR AI 2026, ABS; accepted 11 Aug 2026, page still shows a preprint banner](https://preprints.jmir.org/preprint/93761)). This shows directly that high accuracy can coexist with missing documentary support, and that agreement metrics alone do not detect it. FActScore supplies the method of splitting text into atomic facts and checking each against a knowledge source. Its automated estimator has under 2% error, but only on Wikipedia biographies, not medicine ([Min et al., EMNLP 2023, ABS](https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.741)).

The negative results show that retrieval is not a cure. In BioRAB, an 80% counterfactual corpus cut ChemProt F1 from 86.91 to 47.79. Models' **true negative-awareness rate, their ability to recognize unhelpful retrieved examples, was 0–1.07%**, and no-retrieval models beat RAG on MedMCQA ([Li et al., arXiv; venue unconfirmed, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.08151v2)). For emergency detection in patient portal messages, prompt-only scored F1 0.39, naive RAG 0.59 and knowledge-graph RAG 0.88, with only 41 emergencies. The authors warn that "LLMs may give correct answers through flawed reasoning" ([Liu et al., JAMIA 2025, FT](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12089757/)). A narrative review concludes that RAG effectiveness "depends on the quality, completeness, and recency" of the knowledge source, and lists outdated sources, heterogeneous guideline formats and difficult evaluation as remaining problems ([Gargari & Habibi, Digital Health 2025, FT; narrative review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12059965/)). The Almanac figures (an 18-point factuality gain, 95% vs 0% on adversarial safety prompts, physicians nonetheless preferring ChatGPT 57% of the time) come only from that review and are **SEC** for citation purposes ([Gargari & Habibi, SEC for Almanac](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12059965/)).

The research did not establish three things that the project's design depends on. No clinical study compares BM25-only, dense-only and fused retrieval on **guideline corpora**. **The error rate of an LLM acting as an entailment judge for medical claims** is unquantified. Guideline staleness has not been measured as a failure mode. These are open questions for the project to measure, not settled background.

## Evaluation practice rewards accuracy and ignores uncertainty, so fair comparison must be engineered

### What the field measures, and what it neglects

The strongest quantitative source for 1.2 is a systematic review of **519 studies**. **Only 5% used real patient-care data**, 44.5% tested medical knowledge (mostly licensing exams), question answering made up 84.2% of language tasks against 3.3% for dialogue, **accuracy was the primary dimension in 95.4%**, fairness, bias or toxicity was assessed in 15.8%, deployment in 4.6%, and **calibration or uncertainty in 1.2%** ([Bedi et al., JAMA 2025, ABS via NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10616638)). The DOI (10.1001/jama.2024.21700) came from a news page, so treat it as SEC until checked ([News-Medical, SEC](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20241018/Study-finds-health-care-evaluations-of-large-language-models-lacking-in-real-patient-data-and-bias-assessment.aspx)). MedHELM's clinician-built taxonomy has 121 tasks. In it, **clinical decision support was the second-weakest category** (normalized accuracy 0.56–0.72 in arXiv v2), and agreement between an LLM jury and clinicians was moderate (ICC 0.47, against 0.43 between clinicians) ([Bedi et al., arXiv 2025, ABS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23802)). The *Nature Medicine* 2026 version, as reported by Stanford HAI, lists 37 benchmarks and a CDS range of 0.63–0.77. Cite whichever version is actually read ([Stanford HAI, SEC](https://hai.stanford.edu/news/measuring-what-medical-ai-can-actually-do)).

HealthBench comes closest to the project's five statuses. Of its 5,000 conversations, **21.4% test responding under uncertainty, 11.9% context seeking and 9.6% emergency referrals**, graded against 48,562 physician-written rubric criteria. o3 scored 60%, and the top score on the Hard subset was 32%. The authors acknowledge that "most conversations in HealthBench were synthetically generated" ([Arora et al., OpenAI, PREPRINT, ABS + partial FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.08775v1)). QUEST, from a review of 142 studies, proposes five human-evaluation principles, including **Safety and Harm** and **Trust and Confidence** ([Tam et al., npj Digital Medicine 2024, ABS](https://doaj.org/article/35d95c2463094fad97d777e1d8376e44)). CRAFT-MD makes ten evaluation recommendations, among them open-ended questions, assessing history-taking, and combining automated grading with expert review. Its GPT-4 grader agreed with experts 93.35% of the time, on diagnosis matching only ([Johri et al. 2025, FT](https://gwern.net/doc/ai/nn/transformer/gpt/4/nonfiction/2025-johri.pdf)).

### Datasets and access constraints shape what is feasible

The constraints are concrete. MIMIC-IV-Ext-CDM, the released form of Hager's testbed, requires credentialed PhysioNet access under the **Credentialed Health Data License 1.5.0**, **CITI "Data or Specimens Only Research"** training and a signed DUA ([PhysioNet dataset page, REG](https://www.physionet.org/content/mimic-iv-ext-cdm/)). Students must list a supervisor, not a peer, as reference. Each person signs a DUA for each dataset, and processing takes about 1–2 weeks ([MIMIC access FAQ, REG](https://mimic.mit.edu/docs/faq/how-to-get-access.html)). PhysioNet's September 2025 policy is the newer one. It **strongly recommends locally deployed LLMs** and permits cloud APIs only with **zero data retention, no training use and no human review, all of which must be verified**: "If a service's data handling practices are unclear or cannot be fully verified, do not use the service" ([PhysioNet 2025, REG](https://physionet.org/news/post/llm-responsible-use/)). The April 2023 post prohibited sending data through OpenAI-style APIs and ChatGPT and listed conditional cloud options ([PhysioNet 2023, REG](https://physionet.org/news/post/gpt-responsible-use)). Neither post says whether the newer one replaces the older; treat the 2025 post as current. MedQA (12,723 English questions) is open but always answerable, with no abstention or escalation labels ([Jin et al., Applied Sciences 2021, ABS](https://doaj.org/article/11ebc944223d456caec3066e6152ebd1)).

| MedQA | Partial; needs information-withheld variants to test NEED_MORE_INFO and ABSTAIN | Open; licence not verified; no PHI | Low effort |

**No dataset found has gold labels for all five statuses.** The test set therefore has to be constructed or relabelled, and Chapter 1 should say so. Each label should be traced to a guideline passage or checked by a clinician advisor, and synthetic origin should be named as a limitation.

### Abstention, guardrails and auditability have precedent but not settled metrics

A TACL 2025 survey treats abstention as a way to reduce hallucination and improve safety. It examines abstention from three perspectives (the query, the model and human values) and describes the area as open ([Wen et al., TACL 2025, ABS](https://aclanthology.org/2025.tacl-1.26)). One interpretive mapping, which is an inference: underspecified queries correspond to NEED_MORE_INFO, model limits to ABSTAIN, and safety values to ESCALATE_NOW. Combined with Savage et al.'s finding that verbalized confidence overestimates, this supports **triggering abstention from deterministic rules or sample and agent disagreement, not from the model's self-reported confidence**. In medical safety-critical work, Hakim et al. wrapped an LLM with guardrails that detect anomalous input documents, flag incorrect drug or adverse-event terms and communicate uncertainty, to eliminate specific "never-event" errors in pharmacovigilance. The abstract reports no numbers ([Hakim et al., Scientific Reports 2025, ABS; venue SEC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18322v2)). This is the nearest verified precedent for safety checks placed before and around LLM reasoning, but it is a proof of concept outside decision support. Mökander et al. separate governance, model and application audits. A per-decision log of retrieved evidence, rule firings, agent messages and final status sits at the **application-audit** layer ([Mökander et al., ABS; journal not confirmed](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.08500v2)).

### Fair comparison requires matched information, matched compute and paired tests

The methodological case for three baselines is assembled from general-domain sources. No source prescribes compute matching for medical multi-agent systems. A strongly prompted single agent nearly matches multi-agent discussion ([Wang et al., ACL 2024, ABS](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.331)). Performance rises simply with more sampled calls plus voting ([Li et al., "More Agents Is All You Need," PREPRINT, ABS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05120v1)), so a multi-agent system that makes more calls may be gaining from compute alone. Agent evaluations focus narrowly on accuracy, "SOTA agents are needlessly complex and costly," and cost should be optimized jointly with accuracy on proper holdout sets ([Kapoor et al., "AI Agents That Matter," PREPRINT, ABS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01502v1)). Hager's finding that the order and amount of information alone moves accuracy by up to 18 points means **information access must be held equal** across conditions. These points justify three baselines. A bare LLM tests whether the architecture helps at all. A single agent with the same retrieval and tools tests whether structure helps beyond information access. A compute-matched single agent (self-consistency with the same number of calls or tokens) tests whether gains come from compute. Significance testing in NLP is "often ignored or misused," and a protocol for choosing tests exists ([Dror et al., ACL 2018, ABS](https://aclanthology.org/P18-1128/)). McNemar's test for paired binary outcomes, usually cited to Dietterich (1998), was **not verified** in any methodological source.

The research did not establish that "unsafe-recommendation rate," "must-not-miss recall," "citation precision" or any specific abstention metric (such as risk–coverage or AURC) is a standard clinical-LLM metric. Present them as **the project's own operational definitions**, motivated by the sources above.

## Guidance draws the boundary of decision support but proves nothing about effectiveness

Everything in this section is **REG** or institutional. It defines what responsible, non-device, human-overseen CDS must look like. None of it shows that any CDS improves care, and Chapter 1 must keep it separate from the empirical evidence above.

The **FDA's Clinical Decision Support Software guidance in force is dated January 29, 2026**. It supersedes a January 6, 2026 version and the September 2022 final guidance (docket FDA-2017-D-6569) ([FDA guidance PDF, FT, REG](https://www.fda.gov/media/109618/download); [FDA landing page](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/clinical-decision-support-software)). Software falls outside the device definition only if it meets **all four criteria** of FD&C Act §520(o)(1)(E):

1. It does not process medical images, IVD signals or physiological signal patterns.
2. It displays, analyzes or prints medical information.
3. It supports or provides recommendations to a health care professional (HCP).
4. It enables the HCP to **independently review the basis** of the recommendations, so the HCP does not rely primarily on them.

Under Criterion 3, a specific preventive, diagnostic or treatment directive fails, while option lists and prioritised lists can pass. FDA intends enforcement discretion when only one option is clinically appropriate. Patient- or caregiver-facing support is a device function. Criterion 4 asks for plain-language descriptions of intended use, required inputs, how the algorithm was developed and validated, and patient-specific outputs that show missing, corrupted or unexpected inputs. The guidance names AI/ML only as an example of an algorithm approach and **does not mention LLMs or generative AI**. It defines automation bias as over-reliance on an automated suggestion and says it is more likely under urgency. **One point needs direct checking.** One reading quotes the guidance as saying FDA does not consider functions for "a critical, time-sensitive task or decision" to meet Criterion 4. But the document history (p. 27) records a January 2026 revision that removed a reference to time-critical decision-making on pp. 13–14. Until the PDF is checked, ground ESCALATE_NOW in the automation-bias rationale, not in a claim that time-critical functions categorically fail Criterion 4.

WHO's 2021 guidance sets out six principles. The first is protecting human autonomy: "humans should remain in control of health-care systems and medical decisions." Others are transparency and explainability, and responsibility and accountability with mechanisms "for questioning and for redress" ([WHO news release 2021, ABS; 150-page PDF not read, REG](https://who.int/news/item/28-06-2021-who-issues-first-global-report-on-ai-in-health-and-six-guiding-principles-for-its-design-and-use); [WHO publication page](https://who.int/publications/i/item/9789240029200)). WHO's large multi-modal model guidance was **first released 18 January 2024**, and its landing page now shows 25 March 2025. It makes more than 40 recommendations and warns that LMMs produce "false, inaccurate, biased or incomplete statements" and that automation bias can lead professionals to miss errors. It also notes that "it is not yet proven whether LMMs can accomplish a wide range of tasks and purposes" ([WHO LMM news release 2024, ABS, REG](https://www.who.int/news/item/18-01-2024-who-releases-ai-ethics-and-governance-guidance-for-large-multi-modal-models); [WHO LMM publication page](https://www.who.int/publications-detail-redirect/9789240084759)). This is the main institutional source for LLM-specific risk in 1.1 and 1.2. **NIST AI RMF 1.0** lists seven trustworthiness characteristics and four functions (GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE, MANAGE). MAP 3.5 says human-oversight processes should be "defined, assessed, and documented." The framework is "intended to be voluntary" ([NIST AI 100-1, FT ~93%, REG](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.100-1.pdf)). The Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1) was not verified. **DECIDE-AI** is a consensus reporting guideline with 17 AI-specific items and 10 generic items, built through a modified Delphi with 123 and 138 participants. It covers **early-stage live clinical evaluation**, including human factors, user agreement with the AI, error rates and safety risks. The authors acknowledge a skew toward Europe and the UK ([Vasey et al., BMJ 2022, FT](https://eng.ox.ac.uk/media/11454/2022_bmj_bv.pdf); [BMJ](https://www.bmj.com/content/377/bmj-2022-070904)). The Nature Medicine companion's citation details are unverified.

The Saudi documents ground the scope locally. **SFDA MDS-G010** (v1.0, version date 29/11/2022, issued 3 January 2023) covers AI/ML that diagnoses, manages or predicts disease. Regulation depends on intended use, and **CDS is not named**. The guidance requires clinical evaluation (sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, subgroup and multisite analysis, and comparison of clinicians with and without the device), ISO 14971 risk management, human-oversight controls, a UI that does not unduly influence users, a locked vs adaptive algorithm distinction, and change notification within 10 or 30 days ([SFDA MDS-G010 PDF, FT, REG](https://sfda.gov.sa/sites/default/files/2023-01/MDS-G010ML.pdf)). **SDAIA's AI Ethics Principles** (first edition, September 2023) list seven principles and four risk tiers (minimal, limited, high, unacceptable). They require that "human oversight must be available" over AI systems whose decisions are irreversible or relate to preserving life (p. 20) ([SDAIA principles, FT via an IAU-hosted mirror, REG](https://iauelearning.iau.edu.sa/files/policyandrule/principles_ethics_artificial_intelligence.pdf)). A "2.0" version and a newer Saudi AI risk-management framework appear in law-firm headlines but were not opened ([Baker McKenzie, UNV](https://insightplus.bakermckenzie.com/bm/data-technology/saudi-arabia-saudi-data-and-artificial-intelligence-authority-reveals-ai-ethics-principles-20); [Gowling WLG 2026, UNV](https://gowlingwlg.com/insights-resources/articles/2026/what-saudi-arabias-ai-risk-management-framework-means-for-organisations)).

These documents converge, though no single one prescribes an architecture. FDA Criteria 3–4, WHO principle 1, SDAIA Principle 5 and NIST MAP 3.5 all point to an **HCP-facing, non-autonomous, basis-reviewable system with documented human oversight**. Several scope inferences follow. The project should exclude image and signal inputs to stay consistent with Criterion 1. It should present RECOMMEND outputs as evidence-backed suggestions that the physician reviews, not as directives. It should use a locked model with no online learning. It should describe itself as a research prototype that is not submitted to SFDA. Under SDAIA's tiers, deployed CDS would likely be high-risk; that is an inference, because SDAIA does not list CDS as an example. Chapter 1 can say the design is **"aligned with"** these boundaries. It cannot say the system **"complies with"** them or **"is non-device CDS"**: those are legal determinations the evidence cannot support.

## Research gaps the project can legitimately claim

Each gap below rests on what the reviewed evidence found or failed to find. Word them as "in the literature reviewed" rather than "no study exists." The searches had access limits, and the most important review is a preprint.

The **first and strongest gap is fair comparison**. Clinical multi-agent studies compare against single-pass LLMs or simple prompting. The clinical review found no multi-agent vs single-agent comparison ([Gorenshtein, PREPRINT](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.08.22.25334232.full.pdf)), and general-domain work shows gains shrinking against strong or compute-matched single agents ([Wang ACL](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.331); [Li, PREPRINT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05120v1)). A controlled evaluation with matched information access and matched compute is a legitimate contribution whatever the result. The **second gap is safety-centred, status-level evaluation**. Calibration and uncertainty are measured in 1.2% of studies ([Bedi](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10616638)), no dataset found labels all five statuses, and HealthBench treats context seeking, uncertainty and emergency referral as core behaviours without a closed-option, abstention-capable formulation ([HealthBench, PREPRINT](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.08775v1)). A test set with planted missing-information, red-flag and out-of-scope cases, together with metrics for appropriate NEED_MORE_INFO, ESCALATE_NOW and ABSTAIN behaviour, fills that gap. The **third gap is claim-level verification of decision-support outputs against guideline passages**. Citation-support failures are well documented ([SourceCheckup](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/natcom/v16y2025i1d10.1038_s41467-025-58551-6.html)), but the closest verification study covers a single non-CDS task, risk-of-bias assessment, with BM25 only ([Liang 2026](https://preprints.jmir.org/preprint/93761)). The medical error rate of LLM entailment judges is unquantified. Measuring verifier accuracy itself would add value.

The **fourth gap is robustness of the decision layer**. Order and wording sensitivity of up to 18 points is documented for single LLMs ([Hager](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41591-024-03097-1)). The reviewed literature contains no tests of whether a deterministic decision core over structured facts removes that sensitivity, and no tests on explicitly *conflicting* patient information. Perturbation-based robustness testing is a defensible contribution. The **fifth gap is deterministic orchestration and pre-LLM safety gating**. Centralised coordination contains error amplification in a non-clinical preprint ([Kim, PREPRINT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.08296v2)), and guardrails have a pharmacovigilance precedent ([Hakim](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18322v2)). But a deterministic state-machine orchestrator, and the size of the reduction in unsafe clinical recommendations that rule layers achieve, were not tested in any source found. The **sixth gap is cost and latency reporting**, which clinical agent studies rarely provide ([Gorenshtein](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.08.22.25334232.full.pdf); [Kapoor, PREPRINT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01502v1)). The **seventh gap is operationalisation**. Guidance demands a reviewable basis, human oversight and transparency ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/media/109618/download); [SDAIA](https://iauelearning.iau.edu.sa/files/policyandrule/principles_ethics_artificial_intelligence.pdf)), but no source translates these into concrete architectural mechanisms. The project can claim an *operationalisation* of the boundaries. It cannot claim *compliance*.

## Conclusion

The most useful result of this synthesis concerns how the project fits the literature. Its five output statuses target the behaviours the field measures least: uncertainty (1.2% of evaluations), information seeking, escalation and grounded justification. Its components line up one-to-one with failure modes documented in peer-reviewed full-text studies. The project therefore has a strong, evidence-backed *motivation* while making no claim of *superiority*, and Chapter 1 should be written that way. The literature on multi-agent systems that fail to beat strong baselines helps the thesis rather than threatening it: with a compute-matched, information-matched single-agent baseline, a null result becomes a publishable answer to a question the clinical field has not asked carefully.

Two points that are easy to miss also shape the chapter. First, regulation and evidence meet at one mechanism. FDA Criterion 4 requires that clinicians can independently review the basis of a recommendation, and SourceCheckup and Liang et al. show that citations alone do not provide such a basis. Claim-to-source verification is the bridge between the two, and that is the strongest single argument for the verifier. Second, much of the negative evidence on single LLMs comes from 2023-era models: Llama-2 in Hager, GPT-3.5 in the reference-fabrication study, and the Med-HALT cohort. Chapter 1 should name the model generation behind every limitation it cites and frame the project's own experiments as re-testing those failure modes on current models, rather than assuming they persist.
