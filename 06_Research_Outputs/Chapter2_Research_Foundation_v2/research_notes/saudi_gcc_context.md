# Saudi Arabian and GCC context for LLM-based clinical decision support (theme: saudi_gcc_context)

Scope: (A) peer-reviewed or preprint empirical studies from Saudi Arabia / GCC on CDSS, LLMs or generative AI in clinical care, including Arabic-language medical LLM evaluations; (B) institutional guidance (SFDA, SDAIA, MOH/SHC, WHO, FDA, NIST). **Parts A and B are kept strictly separate.** Part A is evidence about what systems and clinicians do. Part B is what regulators and standards bodies say. Nothing in Part B is evidence that a system works. Nothing here says, or should be read to say, that the proposed prototype complies with any regulation. The prototype is a research artefact, and Part B only describes design expectations it can be compared against.

## Q1. Which peer-reviewed empirical studies from Saudi Arabia/GCC (2020–Nov 2026) evaluate CDSS, LLMs or AI decision support in clinical settings?

### Takeaway
The Saudi/GCC empirical base is dominated by (i) **attitude/readiness surveys** (self-report and convenience samples, high stated enthusiasm alongside strong concerns about accuracy and a clear expectation of human oversight); (ii) **small single-model "ask the chatbot" accuracy studies** (18–220 items, ChatGPT/Gemini, one access date); and (iii) **one large real-world rule-based CDSS log study** showing 95.2% alert override at a Riyadh medical city. I found **no peer-reviewed prospective evaluation, RCT or deployment study of an LLM-based CDSS in a Saudi or GCC hospital**, and no Saudi/GCC study of multi-agent or RAG-grounded clinical LLMs.

### Cited Findings

**A1. Real-world rule-based CDSS (pre-LLM). The strongest local deployment evidence.**
- Alsaidan et al. 2022 (BMC Med Inform Decis Mak 22:101, doi 10.1186/s12911-022-01838-1). Retrospective cross-sectional analysis of system-generated alert reports at King Saud University Medical City, Riyadh (King Khalid University Hospital, 850 beds; King Abdulaziz University Hospital, 250 beds), on Cerner Millennium CPOE with rule-based CDSS, June 2015 to Dec 2017. **5,114,310 medication orders generated 4,446,730 alerts, of which 4,231,743 (95.2%) were overridden** (outpatient 97.8%, inpatient 93.6%). Drug-duplicate alerts made up 79.8% of all alerts (94.7% overridden), dose-range 11.1% (97.8% overridden), drug-interaction 7.2% (97.3% overridden), drug allergy 0.3% (91.3% overridden). **37.7% of overrides had no reason selected.** In a sample of 307 overridden alerts, 80.1% were displayed appropriately and 79.5% were overridden appropriately. Appropriate-override rates were lowest for new drug allergy (43.2%) and drug allergy (62.2%). Limitations: one medical city, one CPOE, a small appropriateness sample, record-only judgement, and no patient-outcome data. — [UCL Discovery full-text PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10147246/1/s12911-022-01838-1.pdf); [Springer record](https://link.springer.com/10.1186/s12911-022-01838-1)

**A2. Clinician readiness and adoption surveys (self-report).**
- Temsah et al. 2023 (Healthcare 11(13):1812, doi 10.3390/healthcare11131812), King Saud University. Online cross-sectional survey, 21 Feb to 6 Mar 2023, **n=1,057 Saudi healthcare workers** (48.8% physicians). Findings: 18.4% had used ChatGPT for healthcare purposes; 50.9% were not familiar with the term; 75.1% were comfortable using it to some degree. Only **39.5% saw value in "providing medical decisions"**, against 65.9% for research aid. Top obstacles: lack of credibility or unknown sources 46.9%, worry about harmful or wrong recommendations 40.2%, medicolegal implications 30.8%. The authors describe trust in ChatGPT-generated medical decisions as "moderate to low". Limitations: convenience sampling, half the sample unfamiliar with the tool, ChatGPT-3.5 in its first three months. — [MDPI](https://mdpi.com/2227-9032/11/13/1812)
- AlGoraini et al. 2025 (Front Public Health 13, doi 10.3389/fpubh.2025.1634638), King Fahad Medical City and Armed Forces Hospital Alhada. Web survey of **n=100 paediatric emergency physicians** across five regions. Findings: 77% agreed AI could assist with differential diagnosis and treatment recommendations; 59% felt comfortable using AI for clinical decision support; 60% agreed ChatGPT's medical answers were generally accurate. Yet **67% believed they could give a better answer than ChatGPT's croup response**, and 66% preferred textbooks for complex topics. Concerns: accuracy 83%, patient safety 56%, ethics/legal 54%, **lack of regulatory guidance 52%**, privacy 34%. Prior AI use was reported inconsistently (79% in the text versus 13% never-users in Fig 1). There was one scenario and one prompt (GPT-4o, April 2024). — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2025.1634638/full)
- Alhazmi 2025 (JMIR Form Res 9:e79730, doi 10.2196/79730), King Abdulaziz University. Online survey, Feb to Apr 2025, **n=201 licensed physicians in KSA** (92.2% of 218 completed). Findings: 25.9% had used AI in the past year; **82.1% said they trusted AI-based recommendations in clinical decision-making**; 63.7% believed AI will always require human oversight; **85.1% agreed patients must be told when AI is involved**; 38.8% thought physicians should be accountable for AI errors; 97% reported at least one barrier. The survey covers AI in general, not LLMs specifically. The analysis is descriptive only. The extraction also flagged several mismatched citations in the paper. — [JMIR Formative](https://formative.jmir.org/2025/1/e79730)
- Contradiction worth stating: stated "trust" ranges from "moderate to low" for ChatGPT decisions in 2023 ([Temsah](https://mdpi.com/2227-9032/11/13/1812)) to 82.1% trust in AI recommendations in 2025 ([Alhazmi](https://formative.jmir.org/2025/1/e79730)). The instruments differ (ChatGPT versus "AI" generically), the samples are convenience samples, and the time points differ, so this is not a measured trend.

**A3. LLM accuracy evaluations by Saudi authors (single model, small item sets).**
- Aljindan et al. 2023 (Cureus 15(9):e45043; PMID 37829968; lighter peer review, see above). **GPT-4 on 220 Saudi Medical Licensing Exam (SMLE)-style questions** across four disciplines: **88.6% overall accuracy**, with lower accuracy on "Hard" items. This is a knowledge exam, not clinical decision support. Abstract only. The DOI was not verified. — [BVS/MEDLINE record](https://pesquisa.bvsalud.org/portal/resource/en/mdl-37829968); [Cureus](https://www.cureus.com/articles/172769-chatgpt-conquers-the-saudi-medical-licensing-exam-exploring-the-accuracy-of-artificial-intelligence-in-medical-knowledge-assessment-and-implications-for-modern-medical-education.pdf)
- Kutbi, Abou-Bakr, Haidar 2025 (JMIR Form Res 9:e84251, doi 10.2196/84251), King Fahad Armed Forces Hospital, Jeddah. **18 questions derived from the 2021 Surviving Sepsis Campaign guideline**, put to GPT-4o and Gemini 1.5 (Dec 2024). Seven blinded intensivists scored on a 3-point scale. **ChatGPT: 5/18 (28%) perfect, 12 (67%) nearly perfect, 1 (5%) low-quality (κ=0.656). Gemini: 3 (17%), 14 (78%), 1 (5%) (κ=0.582).** No significant difference (U=184, P=.48). Safety-relevant errors: ChatGPT contradicted the guideline on VTE prophylaxis and gave an **incorrect renal dose of enoxaparin**, and Gemini gave a misleading procalcitonin-guided antibiotic answer. Both often **omitted time frames and dosing details**. — [JMIR Formative](https://formative.jmir.org/2025/1/e84251)
  - **Integrity flag:** a near-identical article with the same authors, models, 18 questions, raters and numbers appears in Frontiers in Digital Health 2026 (doi per URL 10.3389/fdgth.2026.1672941; accepted 20 Jan 2026). This looks like **possible duplicate publication**. Cite one version, preferably JMIR 2025 as the earlier, and note the overlap. — [Frontiers Digital Health](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/digital-health/articles/10.3389/fdgth.2026.1672941/full)
- Abanmy et al. 2025 (Front Artif Intell 8, doi 10.3389/frai.2025.1550591), King Saud University. Free ChatGPT-3.5 patient medication instructions for tirzepatide, citalopram and apixaban (5 sessions per drug, 15 reports), rated by 4 pharmacy experts and 33 PharmD interns against Micromedex CareNotes. **Experts judged almost all reports incomplete.** Interns were more lenient than experts on correctness and harm. Conclusion: "cannot yet be considered a reliable standalone source". No statistical testing. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/frai.2025.1550591/full)
- Alqahtani et al. 2025 (PLoS ONE 20(2):e0317929, doi 10.1371/journal.pone.0317929). An English MASLD patient questionnaire was translated to Arabic and put to ChatGPT-3.5 (12 Nov 2023). **Ten Saudi native-Arabic-speaking hepatology experts** rated it: accuracy 4.9±0.94 on a 6-point scale, completeness 2.4±0.53 out of 3, comprehensibility 2.74±0.52 out of 3. Agreement was low to moderate (mean Kendall's W 0.22–0.28). There was no English comparison arm. Record or abstract level only. — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/a5be0c67cb12496a89a3d395e1dc521e)

**A4. GCC (non-Saudi) clinical-use evidence.**
- Qunaibi et al. 2026 (JMIR Med Educ 12:e75534, doi 10.2196/75534). This is a multinational study with co-authors affiliated in Qatar (PwC Middle East) and the UAE (Fakeeh University Hospital). It is not GCC-specific: 326 GPs and internists from 39 countries. Design: pre-post test after a 1.5 h "informed AI use" course, with GPT-4 permitted post-course. **Mean score rose from 56.88% to 77.56%; pass rate (≥80%) from 6.4% (21/326) to 58.6% (191/326)**, P<.001, and diagnosis, treatment planning and counselling all improved. There was **no control arm**, so training and AI access are confounded. There was heavy attrition (1,339 of 1,665 eligible did not complete), MCQ vignettes and self-selected participants. — [JMIR Med Educ](https://mededu.jmir.org/2026/1/e75534)
- Kuwait has institutional guidance (Part B) but I found no Kuwaiti, Qatari, Omani or Bahraini empirical CDSS/LLM evaluation this session.

### Inferences
- The 95.2% override rate is the most decision-relevant local finding for the proposed framework. In Saudi tertiary care, **low-specificity interruptive decision support is overwhelmingly ignored**. The framework's ESCALATE_NOW and NEED_MORE_INFO statuses therefore need to be specific, or they risk the same fatigue. RQ1's "unnecessary escalation/abstention" metric has direct local justification.
- The local LLM accuracy studies share the P1/P4 weaknesses flagged elsewhere in Chapter 2. They are static Q&A, use a single model, a single date and no baseline, and do not test information gathering, abstention or robustness. Their recurring failure mode is **omitting dosing and time-critical details and making dosing errors**. That supports the framework's design choice to exclude prescribing and dosing. It is not evidence that the framework works.
- Survey respondents consistently expect human oversight (63.7%), disclosure to patients (85.1%) and regulatory guidance (52% cite its absence). This matches the physician-decides design and the transparency expectations in Part B. These are attitudes, not outcomes.

### Gaps
- No Saudi/GCC peer-reviewed study was found that evaluates an LLM-based CDSS on real patient records, prospectively, or in a deployed workflow. No study was found of multi-agent, RAG-grounded or abstention-capable clinical LLMs in a Saudi setting.
- These items surfaced but were not read (PMC blocked or WebFetch limit): Saudi emergency-physician AI perceptions (PMC12767741); physicians' and nurses' ethical considerations (Nursing Reports 15(9):309; also a preprint at preprints.org 202506.1731); Jeddah physician attitudes (PMC11057398); perception and worries of Saudi HCPs (PMC10473439); clinicians' legal concerns, mixed methods (PMC12249247); a DDI-alert evaluation in a tertiary hospital (PMC10484150, country not confirmed); and JMIR Med Inform 2025 e68044 (page returned empty). Their numbers are not used here.
- MOH Seha Virtual Hospital AI use (an MOH news item on AI X-ray analysis for pilgrims, 30 Jun 2023) is an institutional announcement, not an evaluation. It was not read.

## Q2. Is there evidence on Arabic-language performance of medical LLMs, or on localisation issues relevant to Saudi practice?

### Takeaway
Yes. The evidence comes mostly from **UAE groups (NYU Abu Dhabi, M42 Health)** and is mostly **preprints or workshop papers on MCQ benchmarks**. It consistently shows an **Arabic–English gap that is large for small and medical-tuned open models and small or absent for frontier models**. It also shows weak self-reported confidence in Arabic, heavy tokenisation of Arabic text, and translation pipelines that do not close the gap. Dialect (including Saudi dialect) and code-switching are documented localisation issues, but they are barely evaluated. No study evaluates Arabic clinical decision support on Saudi patient data.

### Cited Findings
- **MedAraBench** (Abu-Daoud et al., NYU Abu Dhabi and Cleveland Clinic Abu Dhabi; arXiv 2602.01714, Feb 2026; listed as an ICLR 2026 poster, track unconfirmed). **24,883 Arabic MCQs** (the paper also gives about 24,000, 24,791 and "25k"), 19 specialties, from digitised regional medical-school exams; test split 4,989; 60.89% first-year items. Zero-shot accuracy: **GPT-o3 0.765, GPT-5 0.764, Claude Sonnet 4 0.694, GPT-4.1 0.673**, DeepSeek-V3 0.620, Llama-3.3-70B 0.547; Arabic-centric Fanar 0.498; medical models **Med42-8B 0.318, Apollo-7B 0.238**; Llama-3.1-8B 0.170, rising to 0.320 with QLoRA. Expert agreement was low (κ 0.152–0.555), and **LLM-as-judge aligned poorly with experts**. MSA only, no dialects, MCQ only. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.01714v1); [ICLR 2026 poster page](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2026/poster/10011856)
- **Cross-lingual evaluation** (Abouzahir, Ma, Habash, Shamout; NYU Abu Dhabi). Peer-reviewed workshop paper, **HeaLing 2026 (ACL), pp. 158–171, doi 10.18653/v1/2026.healing-1.13**; arXiv 2602.05374. MedAraBench test set versus a Google-Translate English version, with English prompts. Letter-MCQ accuracy (Arabic/English): **DeepSeek-V3.2 62.39/62.85 (gap 0.46)**; Llama-3.3-70B 42.10/57.61 (15.51); Mistral-Small-24B 50.25/57.75; Meditron-3-70B 50.51/58.80; **Med42-70B 33.59/53.21 (19.62)**; MedGemma-27B 49.22/52.30. Arabic averages about 2.4 tokens per word against 1.5–1.6 for English. The confidence–accuracy correlation is reported as r=−0.56 (Arabic) and −0.50 (English). The sign is as extracted, and the paper's confidence definition was not checked. The Anthology abstract concludes that self-reported confidence and explanations are weak indicators of correctness. Translations were not manually validated, and 70B models were run 4-bit. — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2026.healing-1.13); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.05374v1)
- **AraHealthQA shared-task benchmarking** (AlDahoul & Zaki, NYU Abu Dhabi; arXiv 2508.15797, no venue stated). 700 MSA items (300 MCQ, including 100 with injected ethical or cultural bias; 400 open-ended, including patient–doctor Q&A). MCQ accuracy: majority vote 77%, **Gemini 2.5 Pro 76%**, GPT-o3 74%, GPT-4o 57%; Arabic-centric models **ALLaM 39%, Falcon-3 36%, Fanar 31%**. Open-ended BERTScore was about 0.84–0.86 for all models, and the authors note BERTScore does not discriminate. There was high prompt sensitivity. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.15797v1)
- **Bridging Language Barriers** (Saadi et al., M42 Health, Abu Dhabi; arXiv 2501.09825; AAAI 2025 copyright line, venue not confirmed). Translated MedQA, MedMCQA, PubMedQA and MMLU. **Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct MedQA 62.0 (En) vs 29.5 (Ar); Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct 78.2 vs 56.6**; best Arabic MedQA was Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct at 76.1. Translate-then-answer pipelines reached 70.5–71.3 on MedQA (LLM translators), against 31.2–40.8 for MT models, still below native English. **Arabic-only fine-tuning of the 8B-Instruct model lowered Arabic MMLU from 46.4 to 30.0**, and fine-tuned 70B-Instruct models rarely beat the baseline. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.09825)
- **Dialect and patient-facing Arabic** (Mohammad, Alkhnbashi, Hammoudeh et al. 2024, Big Data Cogn Comput 8(11):157, doi 10.3390/bdcc8110157; KFUPM Saudi Arabia, MBRU UAE, Birmingham City UK). About 550 Altibbi Q&A pairs across MSA and dialects, **including Saudi** (also Egyptian, Syrian, Sudanese, Moroccan) and code-switched English/French terms. Seven specialists rated ChatGPT-3.5: **86.55% before and 90% after fine-tuning (κ 0.78 to 0.85)**. Accuracy varied by dialect (Egyptian 92% vs Moroccan 85% on fever/headache queries). Fine-tuned outputs **did not reliably match the user's dialect**. ChatGPT answers averaged about 100 words against about 45 for experts. — [MDPI](https://mdpi.com/2504-2289/8/11/157)
- **Calibration and uncertainty in Arabic** (Mariya, single author, India; arXiv 2611.16821, Nov 2026, not peer-reviewed, **non-GCC**). Included because it addresses P2 directly. Qwen2.5-7B: accuracy 0.561 (MedMCQA En) vs 0.430 (MedAraBench Ar); raw ECE 0.386 vs 0.486, and temperature scaling cut it to 0.068 and 0.035. At 90% conformal coverage, singleton sets were 23.4% (En) vs **4.9% (Ar)**. Selective risk on the top 20% most confident answers was 0.098 (En) vs 0.354 (Ar). The benchmarks are **not parallel**, so language and dataset are confounded, and the author notes no fluent Arabic reader checked the items. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.16821)
- The Saudi MASLD study (A3) shows that expert raters judged ChatGPT-3.5's Arabic patient answers to be nearly all correct (4.9/6), with low inter-rater agreement. — [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/a5be0c67cb12496a89a3d395e1dc521e)

### Inferences
- For a Saudi deployment, **language is a reliability variable (P1) and an uncertainty variable (P2)**. Arabic input degrades accuracy and the usefulness of confidence signals for many open models. That argues for (a) testing status and ranking robustness to Arabic or code-switched rewording under RQ3, and (b) not relying on LLM self-confidence for ABSTAIN or NEED_MORE_INFO. The framework's deterministic gates fit this evidence, though the evidence does not test them.
- Saudi Arabia's own Arabic models (ALLaM; Fanar is Qatari) scored far below frontier models on Arabic medical MCQs: ALLaM 39% and Fanar 31% on AraHealthQA, ALLaM-7B evaluated in MedAraBench. "Local model" therefore does not mean "better Arabic clinical performance" on current evidence.
- Clinical notes in Saudi hospitals are often English or code-switched, while patient communication is Arabic. That language mix was not evaluated in any study found.

### Gaps
- No Arabic-language evaluation of LLMs on clinical decision tasks (cases, guideline-concordant management, abstention) and none on Saudi EHR text.
- No study compared performance on Saudi-dialect versus MSA clinical queries with clinician raters beyond the Altibbi study.
- arXiv 2505.03427 and 2507.10216 (the latter with a Saudi-affiliated author, Alasmari; possibly AraMed-related) surfaced but were not read.

## Q3. Current status and content of institutional guidance

### Takeaway
SFDA **MDS-G010 remains v1.0** (version date 29 Nov 2022, posted 3 Jan 2023), and no revision is shown on SFDA's English or Arabic pages. SDAIA's **AI Ethics Principles (Sept 2023)** are in force as principles. A **draft Responsible AI Policy** with four risk tiers was under public consultation and is described in a 4 Nov 2026 law-firm briefing. The **National AI Risk Management Framework** has **conflicting reported dates**: draft consultation in 2025, "published April 2026" per Gowling, "launched/adopted 14 July 2026" per Arab News/SPA and Digital Policy Alert. I could not reach the official SDAIA text. I found **no MOH or Saudi Health Council AI-specific clinical guidance**. Internationally: the FDA CDS guidance was **reissued 29 Jan 2026** (superseding a 6 Jan 2026 version); WHO has the **2021** ethics guidance and the **LMM guidance** (released 18 Jan 2024, catalogue date 25 Mar 2025, same ISBN); NIST has **AI RMF 1.0 (Jan 2023)** and **AI 600-1 (26 Jul 2024)**.

### Cited Findings

Intended use (product specifications, instructions for use, developer information) determines whether a product is a medical device. Classification follows MDS-REQ 1 Annex 5. **It does not explicitly address CDS carve-outs, standalone software categories or generative AI/LLMs.** — [PDF](https://sfda.gov.sa/sites/default/files/2023-01/MDS-G010ML.pdf)
- **Human oversight:** "operational risk controls are features in the software itself that directly interact with the user (e.g., human oversight)". Autonomous systems may need a hand-off strategy to the user. UI design must not unduly influence users. **Trust, learning curve and automation bias** should be evaluated. — [PDF](https://sfda.gov.sa/sites/default/files/2023-01/MDS-G010ML.pdf)

It acknowledges "no internationally aligned framework for the clinical evaluation of AI/ML-based medical devices." — [PDF](https://sfda.gov.sa/sites/default/files/2023-01/MDS-G010ML.pdf)
- **Transparency:** no labelling-content list. "Information to be provided by the Manufacturer" is a technical-documentation item, and usability testing must confirm clarity. **Change management:** significant changes must be notified via GHAD within 10 days, non-significant changes within 30 days. **PMS:** real-world monitoring, PSUR and adverse-event reporting. Data drift is named as a risk. It requires ISO 13485 and ISO 14971/AAMI CR34971. — [PDF](https://sfda.gov.sa/sites/default/files/2023-01/MDS-G010ML.pdf)

### Inferences
- Across all documents read in official form (SFDA, FDA, WHO), three expectations converge for physician-facing decision support: **(1) a clearly stated intended use, user and population; (2) humans keep decision authority, with explicit attention to automation bias; (3) transparency about inputs, the basis of each recommendation, validation and limitations, including missing inputs.** The framework's design (physician decides, closed option sets, NEED_MORE_INFO and ABSTAIN statuses, claim-to-source verification) can be *described as aligned in intent with* these expectations. It must **not** be described as compliant: no conformity assessment, SFDA submission or legal analysis has been done, and the SDAIA texts were only seen second-hand.
- FDA's line that time-critical tasks fail Criterion 4 matters for the ESCALATE_NOW status. In US terms, a function that directs urgent action is the kind FDA treats as device-like. Under Saudi rules, the SFDA's intended-use test (MDS-G010) would decide.
- MDS-G010 predates mainstream clinical LLMs and has no generative-AI or CDS-specific provisions. **There is a regulatory gap for LLM-based CDS in Saudi Arabia.** SDAIA's cross-sector instruments (RAI draft, NAIRMF) are the only Saudi texts reported to address LLMs, and neither, as reported, has health-specific provisions.
- Kuwait's MOH guide, as reported, treats final diagnosis, treatment selection, prescribing, dosing and triage as uses where AI must not be relied on. That is the closest GCC analogue to the framework's no-prescribing/no-dosing scope and physician-decides rule. It is reported through news only.

## Cross-cutting synthesis: what the evidence establishes, contradictions, gaps (P1–P4, RQ1–RQ4), and unverified items

### Takeaway
The regional evidence supports the *need* for cautious, human-in-the-loop, transparent CDS in Saudi Arabia. Clinicians expect oversight, local CDSS alerts are mostly overridden, chatbots omit or misstate dosing and timing, and Arabic degrades model reliability. It provides **no** regional evidence on LLM-based or multi-agent CDS performance in real clinical workflows.

### Cited Findings
(a) **What the evidence establishes**
- Rule-based CDSS at a Saudi medical city suffered extreme alert override: 95.2% of 4.45M alerts, with 37.7% of overrides giving no reason. — [Alsaidan 2022](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10147246/1/s12911-022-01838-1.pdf)
- Saudi clinicians report interest alongside concern about accuracy and safety (accuracy 83%, safety 56%), the absence of regulatory guidance (52%), and an expectation of human oversight (63.7%) and patient disclosure (85.1%). — [AlGoraini 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2025.1634638/full); [Alhazmi 2025](https://formative.jmir.org/2025/1/e79730)
- Saudi single-model evaluations find mostly correct but incomplete answers, with occasional dosing or guideline errors. — [Kutbi 2025](https://formative.jmir.org/2025/1/e84251); [Abanmy 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/frai.2025.1550591/full)
- An Arabic–English gap exists, widest for small and medical-tuned open models (up to 19.6 points for Med42-70B; 32.5 points for Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct on MedQA). — [Abouzahir 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.healing-1.13); [Saadi 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.09825)

(b) **Contradictions**
- Trust: "moderate to low" (2023, ChatGPT) versus 82.1% trusting AI recommendations (2025, generic AI). Different constructs. — [Temsah](https://mdpi.com/2227-9032/11/13/1812); [Alhazmi](https://formative.jmir.org/2025/1/e79730)
- Arabic gap: near zero for DeepSeek-V3.2 (0.46 points) but large for others. Frontier models on MedAraBench score 0.76, while Arabic-centric local models score 0.31–0.50. — [Abouzahir](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.05374v1); [MedAraBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.01714v1); [AlDahoul](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.15797v1)
- Fine-tuning on Arabic helps some configurations and hurts others (8B-Instruct Arabic MMLU 46.4 to 30.0). — [Saadi](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.09825)
- NAIRMF dates conflict across secondary sources (see Q3). Kutbi et al. appears twice in two journals (see A3).

### Inferences
(c) **Gaps relevant to P1–P4 and RQ1–RQ4**
- **P1/RQ3:** no Saudi/GCC study tests robustness to reordering or rewording, or to Arabic and code-switched input, in a decision task. Language variation is an unexamined robustness axis.
- **P2/RQ1:** no regional study measures abstention, escalation or asking for missing information. The only Arabic calibration evidence is a non-GCC preprint on MCQs.
- **P3/RQ2:** no regional study measures citation support or claim verification, and there is no Arabic guideline corpus evaluation. No study uses Saudi guidelines (e.g., MOH or SHC protocols) as the reference standard. Kutbi used the international SSC guideline.
- **P4/RQ4:** all regional LLM studies are single-model, with no matched baselines, no ablations and no cost or latency reporting. There is no regional multi-agent evaluation at all.
- Regulatory: no Saudi guidance specific to generative-AI CDS. MDS-G010 v1.0 predates LLMs, so any claim about Saudi regulatory fit must stay at the level of "design intent aligned with stated principles".
